Marketing has bad press. I used to hate marketing. Trying to find ways to sell a crappy product is precisely why marketing has such a bad reputation. However, when you create a product that you truly think will benefit a lot of people, you want the world to know about it. So how can you do that?

I still hate marketing, by the way. But I hate it because I'm bad at it, not because it's a bad practice per se.

For people like me, developers who would rather code a feature than write content or promote it, I'd say that marketing should primarily be about talking about the product. Let the product do most of the work for you. Talk about what you're building, share it on your social platforms, and let people discover it.

### Be humble

We will never say things like "Monica is the best way to...". No. It's not the best, not the first, not the only. Monica is humble. It lets you do one thing well (i.e., document your life). It's not a revolution. It's a useful tool for a very specific set of people.

We are not redefining human relationships. We are not changing the world. We are not disrupting an industry. We're building a piece of software that helps people remember things about their lives and the people they care about. And that's already pretty cool.

There is something exhausting about the vocabulary used by tech companies these days. Everything is revolutionary, groundbreaking, seamless, or game-changing. Most of the time, it's just a form with a couple of buttons and a database behind it.

Let's call things what they are.

### Write about things that matter

This is why I'll never write blog posts about crappy inspirational things, written by AI solely for SEO purposes. Blog posts will be about the product, how we build it, and things we genuinely care about. Nothing else. Just the thought of having to do a keyword analysis depresses me.

There is already an incredible amount of useless content on the internet. We don't need to contribute to it.

I'd rather have ten articles that people genuinely enjoy reading than a thousand articles designed to rank on Google. If we have nothing interesting to say, we probably shouldn't say anything.

That doesn't mean we don't care about being discoverable. Obviously, we want people to find Monica. But discoverability should be a consequence of producing something useful, not the sole reason for producing it.

### The product is the marketing

The best marketing we can possibly do is to build a good product.

When someone genuinely enjoys using a piece of software, they tend to talk about it. They recommend it to their friends, write about it on their blogs, or mention it in a conversation somewhere on the internet.

We can't manufacture that kind of enthusiasm. We can only build something that deserves it.

Every new feature, every improvement, every interaction with a user, and every piece of documentation contributes to how people perceive Monica. Marketing is not something completely disconnected from building the product. It's a natural extension of it.

### Don't talk like a company

We are people talking to other people.

There is no reason for us to suddenly adopt a completely different vocabulary when we write something on the marketing site. We should write the same way we would explain Monica to someone over dinner.

We can be funny. We can admit that we don't know something. We can talk about things we got wrong. We can even make fun of ourselves.

We don't have a brand voice document explaining which adjectives we are allowed to use. Our personality should naturally come through in everything we do.

### Don't manipulate people

We don't want to create artificial urgency to sell subscriptions. We don't want countdowns, fake discounts, intrusive popups, or emails that make people feel guilty about not using Monica enough.

We don't want to spend our time figuring out how to exploit people's cognitive biases to increase conversion rates by 0.5%.

If someone visits our website, understands what Monica does, sees how much it costs, and decides that it's not for them, that's a perfectly acceptable outcome.

We would rather lose a sale than convince someone to pay for something they don't actually need.

### Not everyone needs Monica

This is probably a terrible thing to say from a marketing perspective, but most people don't need Monica.

Some people have an excellent memory. Some enjoy writing things down in a physical notebook. Some are perfectly happy with their existing tools. Some simply don't feel the need to document their lives.

There is nothing wrong with any of that. We don't need to convince people that they have a problem they didn't know they had.

Our job is to make sure that the people who would genuinely benefit from Monica know that it exists.

### Measure what makes sense

Of course, we need to understand whether people discover the product, use it, and ultimately find enough value in it to pay for it. We need to make money to sustain the project.

But we don't want every decision we make to be dictated by a metric. Not everything that is worth doing can be measured, and not everything that can be measured is worth optimizing.

Sometimes, we'll do something simply because we think it's nice. And we're perfectly comfortable with that.

### Market to yourself

I'll do what you should probably not do. I'll do marketing as if I were the target audience.

I don't like being tracked across the internet. I don't like receiving unsolicited emails. I don't like exaggerated promises. I don't like reading five paragraphs of marketing nonsense before finally understanding what a product actually does.

I like discovering software built by passionate people. I like reading about why they built it, how they built it, and what they want to do with it. I like companies that are transparent about their limitations and don't pretend to have all the answers.

So that's what we'll try to do with Monica.

Will this approach work? We have no idea. It probably goes against a lot of established marketing practices. But we'd rather grow slowly by being ourselves than grow faster by becoming something we don't like.
