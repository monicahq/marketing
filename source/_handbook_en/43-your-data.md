A note in Monica might describe a divorce, an illness, a difficult conversation, or something lovely someone told you over dinner.

To you, it's part of your life. Treating it as raw material for an advertising business would change the relationship between us completely.

The [business model described in this handbook](/en/handbook/money/) is selling access to a hosted service. The personal information inside that service isn't the product we're trying to sell.

### Incentives show up in decisions

If a product depends on extracting more information about people, it has a reason to encourage more disclosure. If it depends on helping people keep useful records, it can be perfectly happy with a short note and an empty field.

I prefer the second arrangement for Monica. There is no reason for us to want a more complete account of your friends' lives than you want to keep yourself.

This is a principle about the business, not a substitute for a privacy policy or a claim that hosting software requires no data processing. Accounts, payments, support, and operating a service all involve concrete details that should be explained accurately.

The same care applies to future features and external services. An exciting integration doesn't make private information less private.

You should be able to understand what you're paying for without wondering whether the real transaction happens behind your back. That's a fairly modest expectation for software that asks you to remember things about people.
