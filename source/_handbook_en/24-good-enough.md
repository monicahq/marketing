There is always another thing to improve. A nicer animation. Another display option. A clever shortcut that will save someone half a second twice a year.

If the condition for releasing a feature is running out of ideas, we're never releasing anything.

For Monica, a useful stopping point is when a person can complete the intended task, understand what happened, and trust that their information hasn't been damaged in the process.

### Define the task before the extras

Take recording a conversation. Can someone enter what matters, associate it with the right person, find it again, and correct a mistake? Those questions deserve an answer before we debate twelve ways to decorate the date.

The [shipping article](/en/handbook/shipping/) draws the essential boundary: incomplete polish is acceptable; compromising security, privacy, or data integrity isn't.

Once that boundary is respected, we should put the feature in people's hands and see where they struggle. A real misunderstanding is more useful than our eighteenth theoretical improvement.

Good enough also allows us to stop after release. A feature that quietly solves its problem doesn't need a redesign to demonstrate that we're still employed by ourselves.

We can revisit it when there's a reason: a recurring frustration, an accessibility problem, a change elsewhere in Monica. Until then, leaving a useful thing alone is legitimate engineering work. It just produces a less impressive screenshot for the weekly update.
