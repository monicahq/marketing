It can be.

That probably isn't the answer a marketing page is supposed to give, but a tool that stores information about other people can be used in uncomfortable ways. A nice interface doesn't settle the question.

Remembering a friend's favourite meal is one thing. Secretly assembling a detailed account of their life is another. The same text field can hold either.

### Purpose matters, but so do limits

For me, Monica began as help with a bad memory. People told me things I cared about, and I forgot them. Writing something down was a way to preserve what I'd been given, not a way to discover what someone hadn't chosen to share.

That distinction matters. So does the sensitivity of what you keep, how long you keep it, and whether you share it.

If imagining the person reading their own record makes you uncomfortable, take the discomfort seriously. It isn't a complete moral test, but it's a useful reason to stop and think.

Don't turn “the app lets me” into “this must be fine”. Software provides capabilities. It doesn't grant permission on behalf of the people described inside it.

[The ethics article](/en/handbook/other-peoples-lives/) discusses the product's responsibility around this. As a user, you have judgment to exercise too.

Monica can help you remember what matters to someone. It shouldn't become an excuse to decide that everything about them belongs in your database.
