Monica began with me forgetting things about people. That makes me a useful first user. It doesn't make me a representative sample of humanity, fortunately for humanity.

The people we're building for want somewhere to keep details, memories, and connections they would otherwise lose track of. They don't all have the same family structure, technical ability, or reason for documenting things.

Some want to remember conversations. Some want to keep family history. Some simply want to stop forgetting a name they have already been told four times.

### Shared need, different lives

What connects those situations is the need to preserve personal context. It isn't a desire to turn friendship into a productivity system.

We should design around that shared need, while allowing room for lives unlike our own. That affects the language we use, what we require people to enter, and which assumptions we make about relationships.

It also gives us boundaries. Someone looking for a sales pipeline, a public social network, or a tool that conducts conversations on their behalf is looking for a different product.

We don't need to argue them into wanting Monica. The [marketing side of this question](/en/handbook/not-for-everyone/) is about explaining that fit honestly.

Here, the practical consequence is product scope. A proposed feature should help these people document what matters to them. Its usefulness to an imaginary customer with an unlimited software budget is less persuasive. We haven't met that customer. They probably already have seventeen dashboards.
