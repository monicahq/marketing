### Inspiration

I've been passionate about web products for many years. Even though I'm in my 40s now, I'm still trying to keep up with all the new technologies and things I enjoy. Over the years, I've seen and read many things that have been a huge source of inspiration.

Here is a condensed list:

- [Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal), from 37signals. An excellent little book on how to create a web application. Even though it was written many years ago, it remains more relevant than ever.
- [PostHog](https://posthog.com). Everything they do feels genuine, simple, and funny. Their approach to building a product, communicating with users, and presenting themselves has been a huge source of inspiration.
- [GitLab's handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook). While I don't necessarily agree with all of GitLab's business and employment practices, their handbook is still a great example of how to document what goes on in a company and share how decisions are made.

Of course, being inspired by a company doesn't mean agreeing with everything it does. We take what makes sense to us and leave the rest. There is no single company out there that has figured everything out.

### Simplicity

Things should be simple. Make decisions for the user, and stick with them. You will not create a great product if there are too many options.

Every time we introduce an option, we ask the user to make a decision. Most of the time, that's a decision we should have made ourselves. After all, we spend considerably more time thinking about how the product should work than most of our users do.

Simplicity doesn't mean that the product should lack powerful features. It means that those features should be easy to understand and use. Making something complex is relatively easy. Making something complex feel simple is incredibly hard.

We shouldn't need extensive documentation to explain how to use a basic feature. If we do, there is probably something wrong with the feature itself.

The same principle applies to the code. We don't want to introduce complex architectural patterns, abstractions, or dependencies just because they are considered best practices somewhere on the internet. Every layer of complexity should solve an actual problem that we have.

Boring technology is often great technology.

### Openness

My own political and ideological considerations have no place in the products I create. Everyone's life is different. People have different beliefs, cultures, values, relationships, and ways of living.

It's not our place to decide what a family should look like, what constitutes a meaningful relationship, or what is important enough to document.

Monica should be equally useful to people whose lives are fundamentally different from ours. We should never assume that the way we live our lives is the way everyone else lives theirs.

This is why Monica doesn't judge. We provide the means to document your life, but we don't get to decide what that life should look like.

### Transparency

We don't want to pretend to be something we're not. We are a small team working on a project we care deeply about. We make mistakes. We sometimes make bad decisions. We change our minds.

There is nothing wrong with that, and there is no reason to hide it.

We want to document why we make certain decisions, not just what those decisions are. Understanding why something was done a certain way is often much more useful than understanding what was done.

This documentation is part of that effort. We'd rather explain how we think than let people speculate about our intentions.

### Independence

We want to build the product we believe in, at our own pace, without having to satisfy imaginary growth targets or chase whatever is fashionable in the tech industry this month.

There is nothing wrong with building a small, profitable, sustainable product. Not every piece of software needs to become a billion-dollar company. Not every market needs to be disrupted.

We don't need millions of users to consider Monica a success. We'd rather have a smaller number of people who genuinely love the product and find value in it.

### Respect

We should respect our users' time, intelligence, privacy, and money.

We shouldn't make people go through five screens when one would do. We shouldn't make it difficult to cancel a subscription. We shouldn't hide important information behind confusing language. We shouldn't use artificial urgency to convince someone to pay for something.

There are many practices in the software industry that have become completely normal despite being fundamentally disrespectful to users. Just because everyone does something doesn't mean we should do it too.

A good rule of thumb is to ask ourselves whether we'd be annoyed if another piece of software did the same thing to us.

### Personality

Software doesn't have to be boring, cold, or overly corporate.

We like humour. We like pandas. We like little details that serve absolutely no functional purpose other than making someone smile. Not everything in a product needs to have a measurable return on investment.

There are actual human beings behind Monica, and we want that to show. We don't need to communicate like a multinational corporation with seventeen layers of management and a legal department reviewing every sentence.

We take the project seriously. We don't necessarily have to take ourselves seriously.

### Enjoyment

Perhaps the most important principle of all: we should enjoy working on this project.

We spend a significant portion of our free time building software. Life is way too short to spend that time working on something we don't enjoy.

Of course, not everything is fun. There are bugs to fix, dependencies to update, servers to maintain, and countless other things that aren't particularly exciting. That's part of building software.

But if the project as a whole stops being enjoyable, something is fundamentally wrong with the way we're approaching it.

We want to build something useful, something we're proud of, and something we would happily use ourselves.

Everything else is secondary.
