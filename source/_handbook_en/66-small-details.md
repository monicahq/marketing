A favourite expression. The name of the café where you kept meeting. The completely unreasonable way someone insisted on making tea.

None of that looks especially important in a form.

Years later, it might be exactly the detail that brings a person or a period of your life back to you.

### Importance changes over time

When we design Monica, we can't decide in advance which parts of someone's life will matter later. A standard list of fields can cover familiar information. It can't anticipate every small thing someone wants to preserve.

That's part of the reason to leave room for personal context and [different kinds of lives](/en/handbook/imperfect-humans/), rather than insist that a complete record looks the same for everyone.

A useful note doesn't have to be impressive. “Always ordered the same soup and complained about it” might tell you more about a shared ritual than a page of formal biography.

But this isn't an instruction to record everything. Keeping a meaningful detail is different from treating every conversation as material to collect. [Restraint](/en/handbook/other-peoples-lives/) still matters.

Sometimes a short sentence is enough. Include the context you'll need to understand it later, and let the rest of the moment be a moment.

I like the idea of Monica holding things that would look unimportant to anyone else. It's your memory. It doesn't need to justify its place by being useful to a wider audience.
