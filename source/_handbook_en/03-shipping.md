---
title: Shipping is better than not shipping
slug: shipping
section: general
order: 3
description: Why releasing an unpolished feature beats holding back a perfect one, and why value matters more than polish.
updated: 2026-11-12
---
What matters most is that the project keeps moving. We should constantly be releasing new features or bug fixes. Releasing things into production boosts morale at every level. There is something extremely satisfying about working on something in the morning and knowing that people on the other side of the planet are using it in the afternoon.

It's easy to stop releasing new stuff frequently. It's tempting. You might want to delay a feature because it's not polished enough. Because it's not complete enough. Because if you wait just a little longer to add this animation, or this design, or this behaviour, it'll be better.

It's a trap.

Most users prefer a functional, unpolished feature over one that hasn't been released. It's all about the value you bring. SaaS companies with lots of VC funding and lots of designers advocate for the perfect user experience. And they are very vocal about spending months on features so that everything is perfect. Yeah. Sure. They have a gazillion dollars to burn on their craft.

We think that, generally, the importance of design and user experience relative to the actual value of a product is greatly overrated. What users truly want is value. If a tool offers enough value, users will endure poor interfaces all day long. On the other hand, the most beautiful piece of software in the world is completely useless if it doesn't solve a problem.

That doesn't mean we don't aim for a good user interface and an overall great user experience. We care deeply about how Monica looks and feels. But this shouldn't get in the way of launching features in production.

There is also a fundamental problem with spending too much time on a feature before releasing it: we don't actually know how people will use it. We can spend weeks thinking about all the possible ways a feature could work, only to realize that people use it in a completely different way. We'd rather spend those weeks improving something based on actual feedback than trying to anticipate everything.

A feature doesn't have to address every possible use case from day one. It needs to address the most important ones. The rest can come later, assuming there is even a need for it. Sometimes, what we thought was the first iteration of a feature turns out to be everything people actually need.

Of course, shipping fast doesn't mean shipping broken software. There are things we can't compromise on. Security, privacy, data integrity, and the ability to access your data are among them. We can live with an ugly button for a few weeks. We can't live with a bug that makes you lose ten years of memories.

We also believe that small, frequent releases are much better than big ones. They are easier to review, easier to test, easier to understand, and easier to fix when something goes wrong. There is no particular reason to hold back ten improvements just because we want to release them all at once.

Not every release has to be exciting, either. Fixing something that's been bothering users for months is sometimes much more valuable than introducing an entirely new feature. Progress isn't always about adding things. Sometimes it's about making what already exists a little bit better.

We don't need a big announcement every time we improve something. We don't need to create artificial excitement around a new version of the product. We just need to keep making Monica better, one change at a time.

Momentum is particularly important for a small open source project like ours. We work on Monica because we enjoy it. Seeing things reach production, seeing people use them, and reading their feedback are a big part of what keeps us motivated. Spending months working on something that no one can use is a great way to lose that motivation.

The beauty of software is that it's never really finished. We can improve things tomorrow, next week, or next year. There is no reason to treat every release as if it were our last opportunity to get things right.

Ship it. Learn from it. Make it better. Repeat.
