We have many other open source projects, and the same question keeps coming up again and again. Why are we crazy enough to release our code as open source?

First of all, most commercial products out there can be replicated relatively easily. This is even more true in the age of AI. Most of them don't have a clear competitive advantage in their code. That's not the case for companies like Stripe, of course, but for the vast majority of products that are essentially CRUD applications, it is. Our secret sauce is not the code itself; it's everything that comes with it: the community, the public image, the ecosystem, and the trust we've built over the years.

Secondly, we strongly believe that we will produce much better code if everyone can take a look at it. When you push code that everyone can read, it puts a kind of pressure on you. Every line of code out there is a testimony to who you are as a developer. So you better make sure that what you put out there is the best it can be and reflects what you believe in. Of course, being open source doesn't automatically make the code good, but it gives everyone the opportunity to make it better.

Thirdly, a product is better when people can contribute to it. Of course, these contributions must be aligned with the values of the project and its direction. When they are, it's amazing. People from all over the world will fix a bug, improve a feature, or add something completely new. And since the project is licensed under the AGPL, it ensures that people who build upon our work cannot simply take it, modify it, offer it as a service, and keep those modifications to themselves.

An open source project also allows for trust and transparency. In the instance we control (i.e., [my.monicahq.com](https://my.monicahq.com)), the code that is in the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/monicahq/monica) is directly pushed to production without any alteration. When your software is about storing personal information, it's even more essential that you have the ability to understand how the underlying system works so you can trust the system that stores your precious data.

We don't expect everyone to read our code. Most people will never look at a single line of it, and that's perfectly fine. What matters is that anyone can. You don't have to blindly trust everything we say about privacy and security. You have the possibility to verify it yourself or rely on people who have the technical knowledge to do so.

Open source also gives you freedom. If you don't like the way we run Monica, you can host it yourself. If you disagree with the direction of the project, you can fork it. If you want to add something that we don't want to add, you can do it yourself. We don't necessarily have to agree on everything for you to benefit from our work.

Being open source doesn't mean that everything should be free of charge. Developing software takes an enormous amount of time, and running a service costs money. We don't see any contradiction between building an open source product and building a sustainable business around it. In fact, making money from the project is probably one of the best ways to ensure that we can continue working on it for many years.

Being open source doesn't mean that we have an obligation to implement every feature that the community asks for, either. We have a vision for the project, and it's our responsibility to protect it. Everyone has the freedom to contribute, just as we have the responsibility to decide what belongs in the project.

There is also something quite beautiful about releasing something into the world that people can learn from. We've learned an enormous amount from reading other people's code over the years. Most of what we know as developers is, in one way or another, the result of someone else deciding to share their work. Making our own work available to others is a way of giving something back.

Last but not least, open source allows a project to be sustained over time by the community, in the event that the founders of the project either lose their motivation or get hit by a bus. In that case, the software has the possibility of outliving its original founders, and this is a good thing.

This is particularly important for Monica. We are asking you to document your life in a piece of software that you might use for decades. The ability to access that information should not depend on whether two developers are still motivated to work on a project on a Sunday afternoon.

Ultimately, we don't want Monica's existence to depend on us. We'd love to see people using and improving Monica long after we've stopped working on it.
