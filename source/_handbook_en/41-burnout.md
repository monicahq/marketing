When a project becomes popular, it can quietly acquire the expectations of a full-time job without replacing the full-time job you already have.

There is always another issue. Another message. Another perfectly reasonable request that would take an evening to understand.

Closing the laptop doesn't close the list. That's a difficult arrangement if you start believing that every unresolved item is a personal failure.

### Motivation needs room

I want Monica to remain useful for years. That requires the people maintaining it to have lives that don't revolve entirely around maintaining it.

The [prioritization article](/en/handbook/prioritize/) talks about enjoying the work. That isn't an embarrassing exception to professional behaviour. For a project built on evenings and weekends, it is part of how the work continues at all.

Boundaries help: saying no to features we don't want to maintain, accepting that replies take time, and leaving some problems unsolved for now. More contributors can help, but reviewing contributions also takes attention. There isn't a magic number of pull requests that makes judgment unnecessary.

Users aren't responsible for managing our lives. We should communicate our limits instead of expecting strangers to infer them.

Likewise, appreciation doesn't need to arrive as a demand for more. A simple message saying something helped can be enough.

I don't want Monica to survive by steadily exhausting the people who care about it. Keeping the project healthy should include keeping enough energy to want to return to it.
