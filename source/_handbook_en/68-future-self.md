Future you will know a lot of things you don't know today. They will also have forgotten why you wrote “that thing with Alex” in a note and apparently considered the job finished.

A small amount of context is a kindness to that person.

What happened? Who was there? Why did it matter to you? You don't need to write a memoir. A few clear sentences can preserve more than a perfectly structured record with no explanation.

### Leave room for the ordinary

Not everything worth remembering is a milestone. An ordinary visit, a conversation on a walk, or the way a familiar place looked can become meaningful later.

Monica began with remembering details about other people, but those details also form part of your own history. The [small things](/en/handbook/small-details/) connect people to places, periods, and experiences.

Dates and names help you find that history again. Your own words help you understand why you kept it.

You can also change your mind about what belongs there. Documenting your life doesn't require preserving every painful detail indefinitely or creating an exhaustive account for an imagined future audience.

And think about [accessibility beyond the application](/en/handbook/leaving/). A memory is more useful when you can still reach and understand it years later.

I like building software that might help someone rediscover an ordinary day they would otherwise have lost. That's a quieter ambition than changing the world. It also feels like a good reason to keep working on Monica.
