There is something ridiculous about software. You sit in front of a computer, type for a while, get annoyed by something you typed yourself, and eventually another person can do something they couldn't do before.

I still think that's pretty cool.

Monica began with a problem in my own life. I forgot names, conversations, and details people had shared with me. Building a place to keep those details was more interesting to me than continuing to be annoyed about forgetting them.

Then other people found it useful. That's a very particular kind of satisfaction. Something that existed only in your head becomes part of somebody else's ordinary day.

### The work itself matters

In [the introduction](/en/handbook/project/), I explain that Monica is also how I learn and unwind outside my day job. That matters. The process isn't merely an unpleasant obstacle between an idea and its commercial potential.

I enjoy figuring out how to represent a relationship properly. I enjoy making a confusing screen less confusing. Sometimes I even enjoy deleting code I was very pleased with last week. Personal growth can be quite rude.

Not every task is satisfying. Dependency updates don't usually produce a spiritual awakening. But enough of the work should feel worthwhile to make the boring parts reasonable.

I don't need Monica to become an empire to justify building it. Making something useful, sharing it, and learning along the way is already a fairly good use of a computer.
